# Claude Hopkins' Advertising Principles

The foundational principles of effective advertising and copywriting, drawn from one of the pioneers of direct response marketing. These principles are not trends-they're rooted in human nature and have endured for over a century, later formalized further in [[breakthrough-advertising-principles]] and [[eugene-schwartz-frameworks]].

## Who Was Claude Hopkins?

Claude Hopkins was a legendary advertising executive who pioneered direct-response advertising in the early 1900s. He tested everything, measured everything, and built a system for making ads work. His autobiography, "My Life in Advertising," is a masterclass in practical marketing. The operator treats his principles as foundational law.

## Core Principle: Test Everything, Judge by Results

Before anything else: don't rely on judgment or opinion. Test and measure.

Hopkins tested every headline, every appeal, every offer. He used keyed coupons to track exactly which ads drove sales. This wasn't theoretical-it was ruthlessly practical.

Key insight: "What is the lesson? It is that none of us can afford to rely on judgment or experience. We must feel our way. New problems require new experience. We must test our undertakings in the most exact way possible. Learn our mistakes and correct them. Watch every appealing lead."

**Application:**
- Keyed coupons / UTM parameters-track every ad, every appeal, every angle
- Test headlines aggressively-"Again and again I have multiplied results from an ad. by eight or ten by a simple change in headline"
- Launch multiple angles simultaneously (see: [[finding-winning-copy-through-testing]])
- Judge by sales, not metrics or personal opinion

## Proof and Sampling Beat Claims Every Time

The most powerful sales tool is letting people experience the product themselves.

You can claim your product is the best. Thousands of competitors make the same claim. People ignore claims.

But show people proof-a sample, a demonstration, testimonials from real users-and they listen.

"A good article is its own best salesman. It is uphill work to sell goods, in print or in person, without samples."

### The Psychology of Free Samples

Samples remove risk: "Ask a person to take a chance on you, and you have a fight. Offer to take a chance on him, and the way is easy."

BUT free offers can cheapen a product: "The 'free' offer cheapens a product. There is a certain resistance when we ask people to afterward pay for a product which came to them first as a gift."

Solution: When offering samples/trials, position it as the company taking the risk, not the customer. "When we ourselves buy the article, just as the consumer does, we show supreme confidence in the belief that the article will please."

**Application:**
- Offer free trials, samples, demonstrations-let people experience the product
- Don't rely on claims alone
- When offering samples, position it as YOUR confidence in the product, not a cheap giveaway
- Use testimonials from real people (landing pages should feature 3-5 customer quotes with specific details)
- Show the product in action-don't just describe it

## Understand Ordinary People, Not Yourself

Most advertising fails because advertisers project their own preferences onto their audience.

Hopkins was blunt: "We must never judge humanity by ourselves. The things we want, the things we like, may appeal to a small minority. The losses occasioned in advertising by venturing on personal preference would easily pay the national debt."

He observed that highly educated people, wealthy people, and people in business make decisions differently than ordinary people. But ordinary people are 95% of your market.

"I know of nothing more ridiculous than gray-haired boards of directors deciding on what housewives want."

### The Ordinary Person's Psychology

Canny, economical, thrifty, suspicious. Not easily fooled on purchases. Responds to service, not flattery. Wants evidence, not opinions. Seeks happiness and solutions, not problems.

**Application:**
- Research your actual customers, not your assumptions
- If you're not your target audience, learn about them (focus groups, interviews, observation)
- Test your copy on real people from your target market before launching
- Avoid sophisticated language, literary style, or appeals that might impress you but bore your audience
- Focus on what ordinary people want: practical solutions, confidence, value

## The Service Mindset: Altruistic vs. Selfish Appeal

The biggest difference between good ads and great ads is mindset.

Good ads say: "Buy my product. It's better than theirs."
Great ads say: "Here's how to solve your problem. I happen to have a tool that helps."

Hopkins: "Argue anything for your own advantage, and people will resist to the limit. But seem unselfishly to consider your customers' desires, and they will naturally flock to you."

"The greatest two faults in advertising lie in boasts and in selfishness."

### Selfish Appeals That Kill

- "Buy from us instead of them"
- Focus on features, not benefits
- Price emphasis without value context
- "We're the best" without proof
- Any language that puts your profit first

### Altruistic Appeals That Work

- "Here's how to solve your problem"
- Service orientation: "We're here to help you achieve X"
- Focus on customer's gain, not your gain
- Offer help even if they don't buy (free samples, advice, tools)
- "Here's what we learned, and how we can help"

People are suspicious of salespeople. They expect you to lie for profit. But when you genuinely seem to be considering THEIR interests, not yours, they lower their defenses.

"Not then, or ever since, have I asked a purchase. That is useless. I have simply offered service."

**Application:**
- Reframe your copy from "buy this" to "solve your problem"
- Lead with what the customer gets, not what you're selling
- Offer genuine help (free samples, guides, consultations)
- Position your product as the solution, not the purpose
- Remove language that screams "I want your money"

## Headlines Are Everything

One strong headline can multiply sales 8-10x. Conversely, a weak headline kills everything downstream.

Hopkins tested hundreds of headlines. He observed clear patterns about what worked.

"Again and again I have multiplied results from an ad. by eight or ten by a simple change in headline."

### Headline Principles

**Specific beats vague:** "Get strong, healthy hair" loses to "Remove dead skin that looks like strawberry bumps"

**Benefit beats feature:** "Contains microvibrations" loses to "Deep clean that's impossible with your hands"

**Curiosity beats claims:** A question that creates a knowledge gap beats a statement

**Problem identification beats generic appeal:** "Lose weight" loses to "Lose belly fat without exercise"

### What Kills Headlines

- Generic superlatives: "The best," "Greatest," "Amazing"
- Feature-focused: talking about how it works, not what it does for you
- Too clever or literary: sophisticated language kills ordinary people's attention
- Too long: one sentence, maximum

**Application:**
- Test multiple headlines, judge by actual sales
- Each headline should identify a problem or create a curiosity gap
- Use specific, concrete language
- Keep it short and punchy
- Remember: the headline is 50% of the ad

## Imagery: Show Solutions, Not Problems

Never picture affliction. Always picture happiness.

This seems obvious, but Hopkins had to fight advertisers who wanted to show the problem to make the solution more dramatic.

"I never referred to disasters. I never pictured the afflicted. Every illustration I ever used showed attractive people and beautiful teeth."

(This was for a toothpaste ad. He could have shown cavities and decay. Instead, he showed beautiful smiles.)

### Why This Works

- People already know the problem (or they wouldn't be reading)
- Showing the problem makes them uncomfortable
- Comfortable, happy imagery is what sticks
- People are seeking happiness, not a reminder of their problems

**Application:**
- Don't show before/after if the "before" is ugly or disturbing
- Focus on the result, not the problem being solved
- Use attractive, happy people in imagery
- Let people infer the problem-your job is to show the solution
- Landing pages should feel aspirational, not depressing

## Voice and Tone: Be Natural, Hide the Hook

Advertising should feel like advice from a friend, not a sales pitch.

Hopkins: "One should be natural and simple. His language should not be conspicuous. In fishing for buyers, as in fishing for bass, one should not reveal the hook."

"Frivolity has no place in advertising. Nor has humor. Spending money is usually serious business."

### What Works

- Conversational tone, not corporate
- Short sentences
- Simple words (common, everyday language)
- No attempt to impress with eloquence
- Earnest, straightforward, honest

### What Doesn't Work

- Flowery language
- Humor (unless your product is fun/lighthearted)
- Attempts to manipulate emotions overtly
- Anything that feels like an "ad"

Modern copywriting has evolved to include UGC (user-generated content) which naturally captures this conversational tone. The principle remains: the ad shouldn't FEEL like an ad.

**Application:**
- Write like you talk
- Use short, punchy sentences
- Remove any words that don't earn their keep
- Avoid corporate jargon
- Test: would you say this to a friend?

## Efficiency and Waste

"If success were easy, the field would be overcrowded. Most success comes through efficiency. Most failures are due to waste."

Hopkins was obsessed with efficiency. Every word had to earn its place. Every dollar had to generate return.

"The difference between profit and loss in advertising is not usually very great."

This means small improvements compound, the same compounding logic behind [[direct-response-advertising-methodology]]. Waste is deadly. Testing and refinement are non-negotiable. You can't brute-force success with big budgets.

**Application:**
- Measure ROI on every campaign
- Kill unprofitable angles quickly
- Scale winners ruthlessly
- Refine winners incrementally
- A 10% improvement in conversion rate is a game-changer

## Personality Over Corporation

People buy from people, not companies.

"People like to deal with men whose names are connected with certain accomplishments. They would rather do that, I have found, than deal with soulless corporations."

Hopkins always created a persona for his brands. Someone the customer could relate to, trust, and do business with.

**Application:**
- Feature the founder or key person in your marketing
- Tell personal stories, not corporate history
- Build a voice and personality for your brand
- Make the company feel human

## The Virtue of Patience and Persistence

Hopkins' final insight in his book is sobering and important:

"I am sure that few men ever entered a business adventure under darker skies. But I want to say here that every great accomplishment of my life has been won against such opposition. Every move that led upward, or to greater happiness or content, has been fought by every friend I had."

"The greatest winnings I have made, in happiness, in money or content, have been accomplished amid almost universal scorn."

Translation: Good ideas face resistance. Profitable businesses take time. Stick with what works even when others doubt.

**Application:**
- Test and iterate relentlessly
- Don't give up on a winning formula because it seems "old"
- Expect resistance to new ideas
- Build on what works, don't abandon it for novelty
- Patience compounds
