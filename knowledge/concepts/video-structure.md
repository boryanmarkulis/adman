# Video structure starts with a show-don't-tell hook

Great videos grab attention by showing a real moment, not explaining it. The best ones start with a specific, relatable scene that mirrors what the viewer already feels.

## The hook is the first act of persuasion

A strong hook doesn't pitch a benefit. It shows a problem or desire in action. The most effective hooks use a clear visual moment tied to a real, shared experience. A comment like "I can't believe I forgot to turn off the light" works because it reflects something many people have felt. When you anchor your hook in something familiar and emotionally true, viewers lean in.

## Show, don't tell, builds instant credibility

Instead of saying "this product saves time," show someone tossing a light onto a bed to reveal clutter, then quickly cleaning it up with one tool. The visual tells the story faster than any caption ever could. This approach cuts through noise and builds trust by proving value in real time.

## Use resonant premises from your market

The most effective hooks come from deep understanding of your audience's daily life. A comment like "I can't believe I forgot to turn off the light" works because it reflects a real, shared experience. When you anchor your hook in something familiar and emotionally true, viewers lean in.

## Follow the hook-beat-CTA structure for short-form videos

The most effective short-form videos use a clear "hook, beat, CTA" pattern. The hook is a bold claim or question that stops the scroll. The beats deliver proof or story through visuals and narration. The CTA drives action. For example: Hook: "This carport in the local market makes electricity." Beats: walk under it, show panels overhead, reveal where the inverter hides, what it powers. CTA: "Comment CARPORT."

## Raw on-site footage outperforms polished production

For solar installers, scraped data shows that raw, unpolished vertical walk-and-talk footage consistently beats highly produced content. Viewers trust real moments on site more than staged scripts. The authenticity of a technician walking through a roof with a phone in hand cuts through skepticism and builds credibility faster.

## Strategic use of bold claims requires intention

Some high-impact claims, like "400% savings," are intentionally placed only in specific ad variants (e.g., Ad B) to allow for independent pause or testing. Even without a documented source, these claims may be retained if they drive performance and align with strategic risk tolerance. The decision to keep them is deliberate and monitored.
