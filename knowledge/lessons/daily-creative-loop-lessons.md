# Daily Creative Loop Lessons

What six weeks of shipping new paid-social creative every day, on several lead-generation accounts, actually taught. The procedure lives in the `ad-loop` skill. This page holds the evidence behind it.

## Choosing what to iterate on

- **Neither spend alone nor cost per result alone picks the control.** Ranking by cost per lead chose ads 7% apart on 5 to 12 leads each, so the winner flipped daily and three rounds were wasted. Switching to spend alone fixed that, then failed the other way: it kept iterating an ad that spent 44 for 1 lead while another spent 34 for 5. Read spend, results and cost per result together, and re-select every run.
- **A 3-day window beats a 7-day window when you ship daily.** The same winner held 72% of spend on 3 days and 34% on 7 days. The longer window kept counting ads that had already been replaced.
- **Break a dynamic-creative ad down to its assets.** One image took 91 to 99% of an ad's spend. One primary text took 82%. A headline that was not the one visible on the post took 96%.
- **Find who it converts before writing a word.** On one account, men 55+ produced 8 of 13 leads while women 65+ took real spend for zero. The next round spoke that segment's words.

## Building the challenger

- **Moving exactly one tiny variable is too literal.** A one-word tweak on a winner rarely beats it. Work out why it wins, then improve the parts that matter, each with a line of evidence. See [[control-campaign-one-variable-testing]] for the discipline this refines.
- **The challenger inherits the winner's look.** Three rounds of new poster styles against a winner that was pure typography taught nothing, because they were different ads, not tests of the winner.
- **Five images should be five emotional angles, not five rewordings.** Rewordings teach wording. Angles teach which need the buyer has. See [[headline-split-test]] and [[ad-variation-framework]].
- **Three tracks, three jobs.** Iterating on the control can never produce a step change by construction. A small-swing track (A) compounds the winner, a middle track (B) says an evidenced angle in new ways, and a discovery track (C) hunts a new reason to act. See [[control-killer-cohort]]: if all the ads look the same, new ones will not get spend.
- **A new picture on the same reason is not a new angle.** One "rates are rising" angle shipped seven times as the big swing in seven different looks. None was a real swing. The angle is the reason to act, not the image.
- **Cheap low-spend results are signals worth developing.** A chat-style ad with no big numbers got the cheapest leads in one account on almost no spend. A dedicated track to develop such signals beats ignoring them because the platform did not fund them.
- **Primary 1 verbatim copies the control's mistakes.** A banned small dollar figure rode along in every round for weeks. Check the held body against the bans.
- **Rotate awareness levels on purpose.** Pain-driven accounts drift to Problem aware. Tagging every round with a level shows the gaps (usually Unaware and Most aware).

## What stops the scroll

- **A real face or a legible number.** Seven of twelve renders in one round were killed for having neither. A prompt that banned faces and numbers "to be safe" produced a whole round of dead images.
- **Show the mechanic, not just the result.** A chalk square drawn on real damage, a handwritten sum on a legal pad, a two-bar chart. The viewer should not have to assemble the argument.
- **Real and a little imperfect beats polished.** A tilted phone photo, messy handwriting, a paper on a kitchen table. Ads that look like content the buyer already consumes outperform ads that look like a brand deck.
- **A comparison is the strongest visual.** Before and after, or this versus that. A hair-brand ad that spent over $100k was one clear before/after. Show the difference in the picture, not in a sentence.
- **One full generated scene beats a stock photo with bars and arrows.** A real-looking moment with a person and tension in the frame, big bold words low on the image.
- **Words on images must be big and bold.** Small text fails a great picture.
- **Name a clear, verifiable enemy.** A specific public cause for the pain (a named rate increase, a named policy, a date) beats a vague frustration.
- **Attraction beats exclusion.** "Bigger bill, faster payback" beat "don't click if your bill is under X". Qualify in the form and the CRM, not in the ad.

## Copy

- **Flow beats the verbatim ratio.** Pasting whole customer sentences from different people read as broken and fake. Lifting 60 to 80% of the words as phrases into one first-person narrator worked.
- **The first line must hook alone.** The feed shows about one line.
- **Headlines over 40 characters get cut.** A 53-character headline did not show on phones. Hook in the first 25.
- **Payoff before mechanism, big numbers before small.** Say what they get in the first five lines. "Up 45% in five years" beats "$2.50 more a month".
- **Chat ads must sound like chat.** Ad sentences cut into bubbles read fake even when every phrase traces to the corpus.
- **Customer quotes supply words, never money numbers.** The account's best-performing ad cited a payback range built from forum comments that mostly predated an incentive ending. The number was dead and nobody noticed. Every money figure now needs a dated fact-gate entry.

## Reading results

- **A tougher lead form changes the numbers on purpose.** Click-to-lead fell from 18.2% to 6.4% after a qualifying form went live, and cost per lead roughly doubled. That was the filter working, not creative failing. Draw a line at any funnel change.
- **Check tracking before blaming creative.** A broken conversion tag reported zero conversions for weeks while leads kept arriving by another route.
- **Low spend on a new ad set is a reading, not a problem.** See [[facebook-cbo-creative-volume]].

## Production

- **Build the shared part once.** For video, render the body and end card once and swap only the hook. That cut render time about 3x.
- **Never let an image model type long or exact words.** Set long text, numbers and anything over a real face in code. Short headlines and labels on a faceless image can come from the model if every word is spelled out and the card is checked.
- **A made-up masthead must be clearly fictitious.** Never impersonate a real outlet, authority or brand.
- **The maker cannot judge its own render.** An arrow landed on a face and a circle sat under its label, both placed on guessed coordinates. A second model checking each card with no stake in it caught both, and a third miss nobody had seen.
- **Review notes are seeds, not rules.** Turning one review into ten hard rules boxed the loop in. Ideas go in the review file; only musts become rules.
