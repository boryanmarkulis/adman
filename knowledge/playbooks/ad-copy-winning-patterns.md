# Ad Copy: What Actually Works

Based on 30+ product tests across ecom, hair extensions, and solar, specific patterns win consistently and the rest are noise.

## Content Before Copy

One thing mentors kept saying and testing keeps confirming: the content itself is 60-80% of the video. Copy matters, but if the footage is weak, staged, or obviously branded, the words don't save it. This is why UGC beats polished brand ads almost every time. Fix the content first, then tighten the words.

## The Core Structure

Hook, body, call to action. Every format, every platform, every product.

The full skeleton:
1. Hook (0-2 seconds): stop the scroll
2. Problem setup: make them feel seen
3. Product features and USP: one or two, not five
4. Demonstration: show it working
5. Desired result: where they end up
6. Call to action: tell them exactly what to do

Nothing in between that doesn't earn its place. "You want to make sure there's no excess information that doesn't need to be there."

## The Hook

Your first 1-2 seconds decide everything. Five types that work:

1. **Problem identification**: "That moment when you realize your brush is full of dead hair"
2. **Curiosity gap**: "This thing is so genius haha!" (they have to know what it is)
3. **Cost contrast**: "I've spent over two grand on salon extensions this year" (sets up the cheaper solution)
4. **Relationship or social stakes**: "My boyfriend said he'd break up with me if I didn't fix my posture" (immediate, personal, creates tension)
5. **Visual surprise**: product doing something unexpected

Losing hooks:
- Feature dumps ("Contains advanced micro-vibrations")
- Generic claims ("Amazing product")
- Telling the viewer how to feel ("You won't believe this")

The contrast hook is especially powerful for mid-to-high ticket items. "I've spent over two grand on salon extensions the past year" beats "get your hero product for $45" because it reframes the price as a fraction of what they're already spending. "It wasn't the $600 I dropped at the salon" builds the same tension. They lean in: okay, so what is it?

**Hook rate is the metric to watch.** A 6% hook rate means the vast majority are bailing in the first two seconds. Winning ads in profitable brands run 30-43%. If your hook rate is low, nothing downstream saves you. Fix the hook first.

For hair ads specifically, "The best secret my hairstylist told me" and "I'm literally speechless, this seamless the hero product looks invisible" have both tested well. Short, punchy, emotionally immediate. One the hero product TikTok test used a male voice specifically to stand out from the wall of female voiceovers in that category. The hook there was explicit: costs 1/20th of salon extensions, looks just as good. Small tactic, but worth noting when your niche has a dominant voice pattern.

## The Body

Address the concern, propose the solution, reinforce with benefits. In that order. Not: features, then you might have this problem, then here's the fix.

Focus on the one or two most important product attributes. If you're running a short ad, there's only room to insert a few pieces of information, so pick the things that matter most: instant volume, seconds to apply, looks real. Not: volume, and it's on sale, and it's easy, and it's cheaper, and free shipping.

Affordability and ease of use convert. BOGO and discounts in the body copy often don't, because you're using prime real estate on the offer instead of the reason to want it. The offer goes in the CTA.

Specificity builds credibility. "Made of Kanekalon, a fiber that is 98% identical to real hair" lands harder than "looks like real hair." A percentage, a dollar figure, a time ("took me seconds") makes the claim feel verifiable. General claims slide off. Specific ones stick.

The avoid generic phrasing rule applies directly here: "internet words" lifted from Reddit reviews and news copy resonate more than paraphrased language. The closer your copy sounds to how real people talk about the problem, the more it lands. See also the target state solar patterns: bill-based backward framing ("my bill went from $180 to $340 over six years") is more credible than future projections because it's grounded in something the viewer can't argue with.

## The Call to Action

Simple, direct, no ambiguity. "Go grab yours before the sale ends." "Buy one get one free, go get yours now." "Link in bio."

Urgency works when it's real: "Last chance to get it before the price increases" gives a reason to act now. "Mother's Day offer ending in" creates the same pull. Price-test hooks anchor the urgency to something concrete.

Countdown timers and shop-now buttons reinforce the CTA when the platform supports it.

## Format

### UGC Wins

Regular person explaining the product naturally beats polished brand ad almost every time. When someone in their bedroom explains why they love something, it's credible. When a professional actor does it, it's obvious.

The UGC formula:
1. Person who has the problem
2. Using the product (real, not staged)
3. Explaining what's happening (casual, conversational)
4. Showing the result
5. Subtle plug at the end ("it's on sale")

**On B-roll:** when pulling footage from outside your own shoots, strip logos and avoid faces. No one can tell where it's from and it reads as a customer using the product, which is exactly what you want.

Use your own content where possible. Borrowing a model's footage works until she finds the ad and isn't happy about it. Own content also means you can iterate freely.

AI voiceovers can give a fresh feel and extend winning scripts without reshooting. Some voices (like Jesse) continue working well across many creatives. Test it when UGC is fatiguing.

**Stitch format** (reacting to someone else's content) has also tested well, especially for social proof or contrarian angles. The Wine Lip Tint winner was a stitch with an attention-grabbing hook followed by a real, honest review.

The Worlds Best Lip Plumper winner was described simply as: "just a girl trying it out and showing her results." It looked the least like an ad. That's the north star for UGC.

### Video Pacing

Cut dead space. Fit clips to the voiceover. Every second not moving the story forward is a second the viewer has to bail. The goal is: hook, clip, clip, clip, CTA, done. No breathing room between scenes that doesn't add something.

Total for ecom: 6 seconds is fine. Longer is only justified if each additional second is doing work.

### Platform Differences

**TikTok/Instagram:**
- UGC format
- 4-6 second spot
- Sound on
- Raw, unpolished feel

**Facebook:**
- Longer copy with multiple angles
- Testimonial/before-after
- Carousel for multiple product angles
- Slightly more polished

**Facebook long-form for high-ticket (solar, 2026):**
- One sentence per line (shift+enter, not paragraph breaks), maximizes above-the-fold lines and slows the scroll
- Hook-only A/B variation: same back-half and CTA, different opening 5 lines, tests the lead without testing the whole ad
- Utility-name variation (the local utility / a named utility / "the target state Utilities") across one proven template extends geographic reach without rewriting the creative

## What Kills Performance

**Frequency creep.** Frequency over 2.0 means you're showing the same people the ad too much. Fresh audience or new creative.

**Self-judgment lines.** "You won't believe this works." "I know it sounds crazy but..." These pre-empt the viewer's own reaction. Cut them and let the product speak.

**Saturation blindness.** Once everyone's running the same product with similar ads, yours is noise. The Deception Ring retested 8 months later: no sales. Market moved on.

**Missing the problem.** "Here's a lash kit" doesn't move people. "Get salon lashes for a third of the price" does. Show them why they need it before you show them what it is.

**BOGO front-loaded.** Putting "buy one get one free" in the hook or body copy instead of the CTA wastes the most important real estate on the offer rather than the reason to want the product.

**Winning on the wrong angle.** The Wifi Outlet winner, by spend, was a girl explaining how it helps her avoid leaving her hair straightener on. It got the most budget but was a peripheral use case, not the core product value. The algorithm picks what converts, not what best represents the product. You still have to judge whether the angle is worth scaling.

## Testing

1. Launch 3-5 different angles
2. Pick the winner by sales, not CTR or CPM
3. Scale the winner, cut losers fast
4. When fatigue hits, test new creatives with the same winning angle
5. When testing variations, change one element at a time: hook, body, or CTA. Not all three. Let it sit 3-5 days minimum before judging.

From the Fidgi Pen: launched 6 creatives on day 1. After day 2, cut 3 for being unprofitable. By day 5, 2 creatives were driving all value. The entertainment angle won the first test. The ADHD angle won the second. "The bored in school angle is king" came out of that run. That's not failure. It's what the process looks like.

**Red flags by metric:**
- Low CTR (under 1%): hook isn't compelling
- Low hook rate (under 15-20%): people are bailing in the first two seconds
- Good CTR, no sales: landing page problem, not the ad
- High frequency, declining ROAS: creative fatigue
- Frequency over 2.0 from day 1: market too small or saturated

## Problem Identification Patterns

The strongest hooks name the problem with specificity and some edge:

"Are you seriously still brushing your hair with that disgusting thing?" (visceral, specific, implied problem)

"Living in the target state without socks, my heels were destroyed" (lifestyle context, severity, relatable audience)

"I have super rough calloused feet" (identification, severity, location)

The viewer nods: that's me. That's the job.

This pattern is amplified by real-world data. In the local market villa solar ads, reframing outdated beliefs with citable proof from the brand's own numbers drives trust and conversion. For example: "It will only take 5.5 years for this project to breakeven. Over the lifetime of the system they'll profit 2.600.000.000 IDR ($158,500 USD). That's a 920% ROI!" Using real metrics from your own data builds credibility far faster than vague promises.

## Outreach as Ad Copy

The same principles apply to direct outreach. Pattern interrupts work because generic pitches are noise.

What doesn't work: "I have a great offer for you." Spray and pray. Asking for time.

What works: do something nobody else will do. Provide value first. A free audit sent as a recording. A lead generated for them with proof it was you. A hand-written letter with a dollar bill stapled to it (Gary Halbert). A personal intro from someone they already trust.

In a sea of generic outreach, doing one thing nobody else bothers with is the hook. Same [[persuasion-psychology-fundamentals]] triggers, different medium.

## The Compounding Effect

After 10, 20, 30 products tested, you develop an instinct for which hook types work in your category, which benefits move people, when saturation hits. That pattern recognition is worth more than any individual viral ad. It's the same compounding [[claude-hopkins-principles]] describes: the person who has run a thousand tests sees angles a beginner can't.
