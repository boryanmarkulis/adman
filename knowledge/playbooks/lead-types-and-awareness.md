# Lead Types by Customer Awareness

Your opening line determines whether the reader keeps going. The type of lead you choose depends entirely on how aware your prospect is of your product and the desire it satisfies, mapped directly from [[eugene-schwartz-frameworks]].

## The Six Lead Types

Every opening falls into one of six categories. Choose based on awareness level.

### 1. The Offer Lead (Most Aware)
**When to use:** Customer knows your product, knows what it does, knows he wants it. He just hasn't bought yet.

**What it does:** States the offer directly. The product. The benefit. The call to action.

**Example headlines:**
- "50% Off This Weekend Only"
- "Get 3 for $29.95"
- "Free Shipping on Orders Over $50"

**Why it works:** No friction needed. Customer is ready. Just make the offer irresistible.

**How to execute:** Be direct. State the benefit. State the offer. Remove friction (free shipping, money-back guarantee). The headline's job is to stop them and make the offer so good they can't refuse.

---

### 2. The Promise Lead (Aware, Skeptical)
**When to use:** Customer knows similar products exist. Knows they work. But doesn't believe YOUR product is better, or you haven't told them about it yet.

**What it does:** Promises a specific, tangible benefit that only your product delivers.

**Example headlines:**
- "Get Salon-Quality Blowouts in Under an Hour" (Drybar)
- "Remove Dead Skin That Looks Like Strawberry Bumps"
- "Lose Belly Fat Without Exercise"

**Why it works:** Directly addresses the desire. Makes it believable because it's specific, not generic.

**How to execute:** State one clear, specific benefit. Not "amazing hair." Be precise: "Blowouts in under an hour." Use concrete language. Avoid superlatives ("best," "greatest"). Let specificity do the work.

---

### 3. The Problem-Solution Lead (Aware of Problem, Unaware of Solution)
**When to use:** Customer recognizes a problem but doesn't know a solution exists, or hasn't realized your product solves it.

**What it does:** Names the problem vividly, then presents your product as the obvious solution.

**Example headlines:**
- "That Moment When You Realize Your Brush Is Full of Dead Hair" (then: here's how to clean it)
- "Shrinks Hemorrhoids Without Surgery"
- "Stops Migraines in Seconds Without Pills"

**Why it works:** Intensifies the problem so the solution feels necessary. Reader nods: "Yes, that's me." Then relief: "There's a solution."

**How to execute:** Paint the problem vividly. Make them feel it. Then present your product as the natural, obvious answer. Don't explain the problem and solution separately. Weave them together.

---

### 4. The Story Lead (Semi-Aware, Looking for Proof)
**When to use:** Customer has heard claims about similar products. Skeptical. Wants social proof that someone like them benefited.

**What it does:** Opens with a short anecdote or moment that proves the benefit is real.

**Example headlines:**
- "I Couldn't Get Out of Bed Until I Tried This"
- "My Dermatologist Was Shocked at the Results"
- "After 10 Years, I Finally Ditched My Prescription"

**Why it works:** Stories bypass skepticism. They show, not tell. Reader thinks: "This person was like me. It worked for them. Maybe it'll work for me."

**How to execute:** Keep it short. One specific moment or outcome. Make it relatable (not a celebrity, an ordinary person). Focus on the transformation or result, not the journey. The story proves the benefit is possible.

---

### 5. The Secret Lead (Low Awareness, Seeking Information)
**When to use:** Customer doesn't know your product exists or that a solution is available. But they're curious. Intrigued.

**What it does:** Creates a knowledge gap. Implies there's something they don't know that will change their life.

**Example headlines:**
- "What Solar Companies Don't Want You to Know"
- "The One Thing Your Dermatologist Won't Tell You"
- "Why Everyone's Switching Away From [Competitor]"
- "The Hidden Reason Most People Fail at [Goal]"

**Why it works:** Curiosity. Fear of missing out. Reader wants to know the secret. They're willing to read on.

**How to execute:** Imply forbidden knowledge or surprising information. Not clickbait (which feels manipulative). Real insight that's genuinely surprising. The secret should be real and deliver in the body copy.

---

### 6. The Proclamation Lead (Completely Unaware)
**When to use:** Customer doesn't know they have the problem. Doesn't admit the need. Or the market is entirely new, with no precedent.

**What it does:** Identifies a group of people and calls them together. Makes them realize they're part of this market.

**Example headlines:**
- "For the Man Who's Tired of Shaving"
- "If You're Still Using Traditional Methods, Read This"
- "To Anyone Who's Ever Felt Overwhelmed by Their Finances"

**Why it works:** Identification. Reader thinks: "That's me." The headline's job isn't to sell the product. It's to get them to read the next sentence. The copy that follows builds the rest.

**How to execute:** Call the right people. Be specific about who you're talking to. Create a category. Make them feel seen. The headline should make the right people nod and the wrong people stop. That's perfect.

## Mapping to Awareness Levels

| Awareness Level | Lead Type | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Most aware (knows product, wants it) | Offer | "50% off this weekend" |
| Aware of product, skeptical | Promise | "Get salon blowouts in 1 hour" |
| Aware of problem, not solution | Problem-Solution | "Remove the strawberry bumps that won't go away" |
| Aware of desire, seeking proof | Story | "After years of failure, this finally worked" |
| Low awareness, intrigued | Secret | "What doctors won't tell you" |
| Completely unaware | Proclamation | "For anyone tired of shaving" |

## Rules for Choosing Your Lead

1. **Match the awareness level, not your preference.** You might love the secret lead, but if your market is already aware, it's wasted effort.

2. **One lead type per ad.** Don't mix an offer with a story. Pick one and execute it fully.

3. **Load your lead with specificity.** Generic claims lose. "Amazing results" vs. "Results in 72 hours." Specific wins.

4. **Your lead's job is not to sell.** Its job is to compel the reader to read the next sentence. Everything else follows from that.

5. **Test lead types, not products.** If an offer lead isn't working, don't change the product. Try a promise lead. Try a story lead. Audience awareness matters more than product quality.

## The 80/20 of Headlines

Spend 80% of your time on the lead. Iterate it. Test variations. The lead determines success more than any other element of the ad.

If your overall ad isn't working, the lead is probably the culprit, not the body copy.

## Common Lead Mistakes

**Using offer when market is unaware:** You can't sell what people don't know they want. Use proclamation or secret instead.

**Mixing multiple lead types:** Confuses the reader. One clear message per ad.

**Being too clever:** Clever leads work for sophisticated, self-aware audiences. Most audiences want clarity.

**Leaning on superlatives:** "Best," "Greatest," "Amazing." These are weak. Show, don't claim.

**Forgetting the next sentence:** Your lead's only job is to make them read the next sentence. If they stop after the headline, everything else fails.
