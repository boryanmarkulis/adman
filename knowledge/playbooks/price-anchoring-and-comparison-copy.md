# Price Anchoring and Comparison Copy

The most powerful way to make a price feel affordable: compare it to a painful alternative the customer already knows.

## The Pattern

The beauty brand's the hero product ads follow a consistent structure:

1. **Name the anchor** (the expensive alternative): "$600 salon extensions" or "$1600 per year in salon maintenance"
2. **Shock with the contrast**: "I actually spent under $50"
3. **Reinforce the comparison**: "Why would I spend $1000+ at the salon when this costs under $50?"

This is not discounting. This is reframing what "expensive" means, the same value-stack logic [[grand-slam-offer]] runs on.

## Why It Works

Price is relative. $50 feels cheap when compared to $600. It feels expensive when compared to $20.

The customer's reference point determines their perception of value. Your job: set that reference point before stating your price.

### The Psychology

When you anchor to a higher price first, the customer's brain uses that as the baseline. Everything lower feels like a steal. This is Cialdini's anchoring principle applied to copy.

## The Beauty Brand's Framework

### Hook Pattern: The Bet

"I'd bet $100 you can't guess how much I spent on these extensions."

Then reveal: "Hint: it wasn't the usual $600 I drop at the salon. Nope, I actually found this the hero product for under $50."

**Why it works:** The bet creates curiosity. The reveal creates relief. The customer feels smart for getting a good deal.

### The Comparison Stack

The beauty brand consistently stacks comparisons:
- **Time**: "60 seconds to put in" vs salon "hours every month"
- **Cost**: "Under $50" vs salon "$600 per appointment"
- **Maintenance**: "Take it off whenever" vs salon "$150 month ongoing"
- **Total**: "$1600 per year" vs "under $50 one time"

Each comparison reinforces that the hero product is the obvious choice.

### The Annual Cost Calculation

"I spent over $2,000 on salon extensions in the past year. It adds up so fast when you think about it - $600 to get them done and another $150 a month to maintain them!"

This is powerful because it makes the customer do math. They realize they're already spending this. the hero product isn't an expense, it's a savings.

## Hook Variations Using Price Anchoring

**High anchor with shock reveal:**
"They want you to think having beautiful hair is expensive. But this the hero product costs under $50."

**Annual cost framing:**
"Did you know the total cost of salon extensions is $1650 a year? No way. I got my dream hair for one-time $50."

**Specific comparison:**
"My girlfriends spent $600 at the salon. I spent $50 on the hero product and it looks just as good."

**The jealousy angle:**
"They all thought I spent $1000 on this look. But I actually spent less than $50."

## Where This Framework Works

### Best For:
- Products with an expensive alternative (salon services, professional tools, recurring subscriptions)
- Markets where the customer already pays a painful price (they know what $600 extensions cost)
- Emotional purchases where price guilt is a factor (if customers feel bad spending money, anchor to what they're NOT spending)

### Avoid When:
- No known expensive alternative exists (hard to anchor)
- Customer is unfamiliar with the category (they don't know what salon extensions cost)
- Price is a side benefit, not the main benefit (focus on the primary value first)

## The Math of Comparison

The beauty brand's actual math from the ads:
- Salon: $600 installation + $150/month maintenance = $1800/year
- The beauty brand: $50 (or $25 with buy 1 get 1 free = $25 per the hero product for two)
- Savings: $1750/year

This number is so stark that it becomes the headline. When you calculate it out, the comparison does the selling.

## Combining Price Anchoring with Other Frameworks

**Price Anchoring + Problem-Solution:**
"Salon extensions cost $600 and damage your hair. The beauty brand's the hero product costs under $50 and doesn't damage anything."

You're anchoring to price AND addressing an objection (damage).

**Price Anchoring + Specificity:**
"Instead of $600 at the salon, I got this $45 the hero product and put it in myself in 60 seconds."

Every number is specific. The contrast is undeniable.

**Price Anchoring + Testimonial:**
"I was going to spend $1000 on salon extensions. I'm so glad I didn't. This the hero product cost $50 and works better!"

The relief in the testimonial is the anchor working. The customer just saved $950.

## Implementation Rules

1. **Anchor must be credible.** $600 for salon extensions is real. Customers know this. Don't inflate it.

2. **Anchor must be painful.** The comparison only works if the original price makes the customer wince. "Costs $600" triggers pain. "Costs $50" triggers relief.

3. **Calculate total cost, not just per-unit.** "$150/month for a year" ($1800) is more shocking than "$150/month."

4. **Reveal the deal quickly.** Don't make them wait. Hook (the anchor) → Reveal (your price) → Benefit.

5. **Reinforce with language.** "Under $50" feels cheaper than "$49.95." "Less than $600" vs "$599" - round numbers feel bigger.

## Testing This Framework

When testing price anchoring, test variations of:
1. **The anchor amount** - Try $600 vs $800 vs $1200
2. **The anchor duration** - Try per-visit vs per-month vs per-year
3. **The reveal phrasing** - Try "under $50" vs "$45" vs "just $50"
4. **The justification** - Why is the alternative so expensive? Make that clear.
