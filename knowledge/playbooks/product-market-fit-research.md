# Product-Market Fit Research

The job is to find demand that already exists, not manufacture it. Every technique here is about reading the market, not convincing yourself.

## Daily Signal Collection

A good research loop is boring on purpose. The operator's pattern was steady, not random:

- 45 minutes of product research every day
- Scroll TikTok FYP and similar feeds deliberately
- Do not interact with dropshipping stores
- Interact with ads, comments, and things people actually react to
- Search Amazon reviews for long, detailed complaints and adjacent purchases
- Move every candidate into a tracking sheet after the first pass

The point is to train your eye on actual demand, not your own taste.

## Product Filters

A product is worth testing when most of this is true:

- 3x margin
- Plenty of content to cut into ads
- Demand already visible on Amazon or in comments
- Solves a real pain, emotional or physical
- Sparks an immediate reaction like "fuck yes"
- Broad enough to matter, not just a tiny curiosity
- Easy to understand in one visual
- No obvious saturation unless the angle is genuinely different

If you cannot see a strong reason people already want it, keep moving. The same filter applies in [[product-test-selection-heuristics]].

## Features, Benefits, and Angles

These three things are different and need to be separated before you write a word of copy.

Features are physical facts: heat-friendly, synthetic, adjustable. Benefits are what those facts mean to the customer: comfortable, holds well, doesn't damage hair. The customer buys the benefit, not the feature. "Feels light" is a feature. "You'll forget you're wearing it" is a benefit.

Angles are the broader selling themes you can build a campaign around. The most convenient option. The most affordable in its category. The one that gets you compliments. Each angle can generate multiple ads. Finding three or four solid angles before you start producing is what keeps your creative tests from being random. See [[ad-creative-strategy]] for how to execute once you have them.

## Competitor Analysis

Studying what competitors do is table stakes, but it only helps if you extract the right things. Look at what copy formats they run, what hooks they lean on, what objections their comments surface. Then use that to build clear instructions before you brief creators or write anything. Vague briefs produce vague content. The research phase is when you remove vagueness.

## Brand Legitimacy as a Product Filter

A product can have good demand and still fail if the brand presentation undermines trust. Logo quality, product images, and the overall look of the store affect conversion as much as the offer does. If the product is inherently hard to position, that's a real cost. Factor it in before you commit to testing. A strong story can carry a strange product, but it cannot carry a sketchy-looking one. See also [[product-market-fit]] and [[social-proof]].

## Storytelling as a Signal

Products with a pre-existing story in the culture tend to be easier to sell. The Thai deodorant crystal is a good example: Thai people had been using it for generations before Western brands started selling synthetic alternatives. That story does the credibility work before the ad even gets to the offer. When a product has that kind of origin or contrast baked in, it is easier to make people feel like they discovered something real rather than being sold to.

## Secondary Products

Secondary products are sourced from AliExpress, vetted through a sourcing agent, and built into simple product pages for testing as upsells. The focus is on organizing them on-site and feeding them to email marketers. A spreadsheet tracks each candidate with link, price, and cost of goods. The bar for secondary products is lower than for a main offer, but they still need margin and a plausible reason someone would want them alongside what they already bought. See also secondary products.

## Test Fast

The operator's operating cadence was simple:

- 1 to 2 products per week if the signal is there
- About 2 hours a day to get a test live
- 3 strong creatives and copies per product minimum
- 10 to 30 second creatives with clean B-roll
- Hook first, AIDA as a loose outline after

The test is not supposed to be precious. It is supposed to tell you whether the idea deserves more time.

## Decide Quickly

- Launch small ABO tests with broad targeting unless you have a real reason not to
- If it is not profitable day one, cut it and move on
- If it is profitable day one, let it breathe on day two and build from the winner
- Move winning ads into a bigger launch only after they prove they are actually paying
- If a fixed block of tests fails, stop burning time and either change the game or get help

The whole point is to avoid falling in love with weak products. The same discipline [[control-killer-cohort]] runs on. See also [[product-market-fit-before-scale]] for when to stop testing and commit.

## Audience Signals

Real demand shows up in how people talk about their problems. For example, the target audience for a shoe product includes individuals concerned about sustainability and seeking carbon-neutral, comfortable footwear. Similarly, car buyers are often looking for reliability and are willing to invest in quality over price. These signals come from real pain points, not hypothetical personas. Use them to guide your messaging and angle selection.

## Market Realities

Some products carry inherent skepticism. Salon extensions, for instance, are widely seen as a scam of the 21st century due to their damaging effects on hair and inconsistent quality. This perception is a real barrier that must be addressed in copy and visuals. The same applies to any product with a history of poor user experience. If the market already distrusts it, you need a stronger story or proof to overcome that.

## Messaging and Trust

A campaign's success hinges on clear messaging, trust-building elements like guarantees and free shipping, and avoiding misleading claims that could trigger ad restrictions. The most effective offers don't just sell a product; they reframe the customer's reality. For example, positioning a ring company as honest by revealing that other brands have been overcharging helps build credibility fast. Transparency becomes a competitive advantage.
